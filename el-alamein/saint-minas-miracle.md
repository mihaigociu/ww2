## 1. Introduction — Two Ways of Telling the Same Story

The Second Battle of El-Alamein (23 October – 4 November 1942) is one of the most thoroughly documented battles in military history. Historians have analysed Montgomery's tactics, Rommel's supply crisis, the Ultra intelligence advantage, and the roles of Australian, New Zealand, South African, Indian, Greek, and British soldiers in extraordinary detail. The battle's outcome is not in doubt.

But alongside the military history, another story has been told — by Greek Orthodox and Coptic Christian communities — for more than eighty years. In their tradition, the Allied victory was not won by guns and tanks alone. On the first night of the offensive, the ancient martyred soldier-saint of Egypt appeared in the desert and struck terror into the enemy lines.

This document explores that story: who Saint Minas was, why the battle was fought near his sacred burial ground, what the tradition says happened on the night of 23–24 October 1942, and how the story has been passed down. It also asks an important question that historians and students should always ask: how do we read a story that blends historical events with religious belief?

There are no simple answers. But the questions themselves are worth taking seriously.

## 2. Who Was Saint Minas?

![Christ and Saint Menas — 6th century Coptic icon from Bawit, Egypt, now in the Louvre](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0f/Menas.jpg)
*A 6th-century Coptic icon from Bawit, Egypt, depicting Christ with his arm around Saint Menas. One of the most famous early Christian images from Egypt. (Public Domain — Louvre Museum, Paris)*

Saint Minas (also spelled Menas, Mena, or Mina) was a real person, born in Egypt around **285 AD**. He joined the Roman army as a teenager and was posted to **Cotyaeum** in Phrygia — a region of what is now west-central Turkey — where he served under a Roman commander during the reign of Emperor Diocletian.

The early fourth century was a dangerous time to be a Christian. Emperor Diocletian launched one of history's most severe persecutions of the new faith, ordering Christians to renounce their beliefs or face imprisonment, torture, and death. Roman soldiers were expected to take part in pagan religious ceremonies as part of their military duties.

Minas refused. He left his unit and retreated into the nearby desert, living as an **ascetic** — a hermit who fasted and prayed in solitude. According to tradition, he had a vision there of angels placing crowns on the heads of Christian martyrs, which inspired him to return to public life and openly declare his faith.

He walked back into Cotyaeum during a public festival, stood before the crowd, and announced that he was a Christian. He was arrested immediately. After enduring prolonged torture with extraordinary composure, he was **beheaded by the sword**, then his body was burned. The date of his martyrdom is given as approximately **296–304 AD** — the exact year varies between Coptic and Greek Orthodox sources.

### The Legend of the Camels

The story of how Saint Minas came home to Egypt is as famous as his death. Fellow Christians recovered his body and placed it on a camel to transport it back to Egypt. The camel was led westward from Phrygia into the desert southwest of Alexandria. At a certain point in the desert wilderness, the camel **refused to move any further**. The attendants interpreted this as a divine sign. They buried the body where the camel had stopped — and that spot, near the southern shore of Lake Mariout (Lake Mareotis), became one of the most sacred places in all of early Christianity.

This image of a holy man flanked by two camels — the burial legend made visual — became the defining symbol of Saint Minas across all Christian traditions. It appears on his icons, on the pilgrim flasks that spread his fame across the Mediterranean, and in the frescoes of his ruined desert church. It is also central to what the tradition says happened at El-Alamein in 1942.

![Pilgrim flask (ampulla) with Saint Menas flanked by two camels — 6th–8th century, Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/53/Ampulla_%28Flask%29_with_Saint_Menas_MET_DP258369.jpg)
*An earthenware pilgrim flask (ampulla) from the 6th–8th century, showing Saint Menas standing between two kneeling camels — his signature iconographic image. These flasks were carried home by pilgrims from Abu Mena across the entire Mediterranean world. (CC0 Public Domain — Metropolitan Museum of Art)*

## 3. The Shrine of Abu Mena — Sacred Ground

The burial site of Saint Minas did not remain forgotten for long. Within decades of his death, Christians were making pilgrimages to the spot in the Egyptian desert. By the late 4th century, **Emperor Arcadius** had built a great basilica over the tomb. By the 5th and 6th centuries, the complex had grown into one of the largest Christian pilgrimage centres in the world — a full city of churches, monasteries, Roman baths, hostels, workshops, and streets, called **Martyroupolis** ("City of the Martyr").

The site is known in Arabic as **Abu Mena** — "Father Mena" or "the place of Mena." It lies approximately **50 kilometres southwest of Alexandria**, in the desert near the shore of Lake Mariout in Egypt's Western Desert.

![Map of the northwestern Egyptian Mediterranean coast, with El-Alamein marked in red](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4f/Al_alamayn_map.png)
*The northwestern Egyptian coast: El-Alamein (marked with a red dot) lies approximately 106 km west of Alexandria. Abu Mena — Saint Minas's burial site — lies roughly 50 km southwest of Alexandria, in the same desert corridor between the two locations. The inset shows the position of this region within Egypt. (Public Domain — United Nations cartography)*

### Pilgrims from Across the World

The reach of Saint Minas's cult is one of the most remarkable stories in early Christian history. Pilgrims came from Egypt, Sudan, the Middle East, Greece, Italy, France, and even Britain to seek healing and blessing at his shrine. They carried home small clay flasks — called **ampullae** — stamped with his image, filled with holy water or blessed oil. Archaeologists have found these Menas flasks as far away as:

- Heidelberg, Germany
- Marseille, France
- Dalmatia (modern Croatia)
- Jerusalem
- Meols, Cheshire, England

Each flask that survives is evidence of a journey — a sick or desperate person who crossed the Mediterranean to pray at the tomb of an Egyptian soldier-saint, and carried a little of that holiness home.

### Ruins in the Desert

The Arab conquest of Egypt in the 7th century ended the great pilgrimage era. The basilica complex fell into ruin and was gradually buried by desert sand. It was only rediscovered by archaeologists between 1905 and 1907.

![Ruins of the Basilica of the Crypt at Abu Mena, the main church built over Saint Menas's tomb](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/45/Basilica_of_The_Crypt_at_Abu_Mena_%28V%29.jpg)
*The ruins of the Basilica of the Crypt at Abu Mena — the great church built over Saint Menas's tomb. In the 1940s, these ruins lay in the desert southwest of Alexandria. (CC BY 2.0 — Iris Fernandez / ISAW-NYU)*

Today, Abu Mena is a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** (inscribed 1979). The ruins of the ancient basilica, churches, and Roman baths are still visible. A living monastic community has returned to the site, and it remains a place of pilgrimage for Coptic Orthodox Christians.

![Ruins of the bath complex at Abu Mena pilgrimage site, Egypt](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/d8/Ruins_at_Abu_Mena_%28VI%29.jpg)
*The ancient bath complex at Abu Mena, part of the vast Late Antique pilgrimage city. The site lay in ruins in 1942, surrounded by the same Western Desert where the battle of El-Alamein was fought. (CC BY 2.0 — Iris Fernandez / ISAW-NYU)*

## 4. A Name in the Sand: El-Alamein and the Saint

The name **El-Alamein** comes from the Arabic **العلمين** (*al-ʿAlamayn*) — the dual form of *ʿalam*, meaning "flag," "marker," or "landmark." Literally: **"the two landmarks."** The name was given to the area because of two ancient cairns or twin hilltops that served as navigation reference points in the otherwise featureless desert.

Some religious sources offer a complementary reading: that the "two landmarks" referred to were structures or monuments associated with the sacred precinct of Saint Menas, whose burial ground lay not far to the east. In this interpretation, the very name of the place carries the saint's presence — as if the desert had always known whose ground this was.

Whether or not that etymology is linguistically precise, the geographical fact is clear: **El-Alamein and Abu Mena are in the same corridor of the Egyptian Western Desert**, between Alexandria and the Libyan border. When Rommel's Panzerarmee Afrika was halted at El-Alamein in the summer of 1942, it was halted in a landscape saturated with the memory of one of Egypt's most beloved saints — and close to the ruins of the great church built in his honour more than fifteen centuries earlier.

## 5. Greece in Africa: The Greek Brigade at El-Alamein

Before examining the miracle tradition, it is important to establish one key historical fact: **Greek soldiers really were at El-Alamein**.

After Germany invaded and occupied Greece in April 1941, the Greek government and armed forces went into exile. Greek soldiers who escaped the occupation formed units that fought alongside the British throughout the Middle East campaign. The **1st Greek Infantry Brigade** — part of the British Eighth Army — participated in the **Second Battle of El-Alamein** in October–November 1942.

The Greek brigade fought under the British 50th Infantry Division and suffered real casualties: **89 killed and 228 wounded** during the battle.

![Greek artillery in action during the Second Battle of El-Alamein, 1942](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b0/GREEK-ART-ALAMEIN.jpg)
*Greek artillery in action at El-Alamein, 1942. The 1st Greek Infantry Brigade was a genuine part of the Allied force, fighting to liberate their occupied homeland and protect Egypt from Axis conquest. (Public Domain)*

The Greek soldiers at El-Alamein were fighting for multiple reasons at once: they were serving the Allied cause, but they were also fighting for their own country — which was under brutal Nazi occupation. Many were deeply religious Greek Orthodox Christians. Alexandria, just over 100 kilometres to the east, had a large and thriving Greek community. The **Patriarchate of Alexandria** — the Greek Orthodox institution responsible for Egypt — was actively engaged in the life of that community throughout the war.

This context matters. The miracle tradition did not emerge in a vacuum. It emerged from a community of faith that had soldiers in the field, family members in occupied Greece, and deep roots in the sacred landscape of the Egyptian desert.

![The Greek War Cemetery at El-Alamein, Egypt](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e8/AlameinGreekCemet1.jpg)
*The Greek War Cemetery at El-Alamein, where soldiers of the 1st Greek Brigade who fell in the North African campaign are buried. (CC BY-SA 3.0 — Roland Unger)*

## 6. The Miracle: What the Tradition Says

The religious tradition — preserved in Greek Orthodox and Coptic Orthodox communities, and first written down in a Greek-language book by **Archimandrite Charalambos Vassilopoulos** titled *The Victorious in El Alamein* — tells the following story.

On the **first night of the Allied offensive** — the night of **23–24 October 1942** — at **midnight**, while the largest artillery barrage since the First World War was shaking the desert and Allied infantry were advancing through the minefields, something else happened.

From the ruins of the ancient church of Saint Menas — standing in the desert near the battle lines — a figure appeared. He was recognized as Saint Minas himself, appearing exactly as he is depicted in the frescoes of his church and on the ancient pilgrim flasks: a soldier-saint **leading a caravan of camels**. He advanced toward the German and Italian camp.

According to the tradition, the Axis soldiers were overcome by sudden **terror and confusion**. They fled in panic. The apparition is presented in religious sources as a direct supernatural intervention that contributed to the breaking of Axis morale and the Allied victory that followed.

Greek Orthodox sources specify that the apparition was witnessed by **Greek soldiers in the Allied lines** — men who would have been especially familiar with the saint, whose feast is celebrated across the Greek world every year.

### The Montgomery Dream

A second strand of the tradition, found primarily on the website *stminaalamein.com*, describes a **dream** that General Montgomery supposedly had before the decisive battle. In this account, he saw a figure pointing toward the German lines, and wherever the figure pointed, German forces were routed. When Montgomery asked the figure's name, he replied: **"Mena."**

This story is presented on some religious websites as if it comes from Montgomery's own writings. However, **no such account appears in Montgomery's published memoirs** (1958) or in any secular biography. Montgomery was a man of deep personal faith, but his writings focus on strategy and command. The dream account cannot be verified from any primary source, and it should be read as part of the devotional tradition rather than as documented history.

## 7. The Feast Day: 11 November 1942

One of the most historically grounded elements of the whole tradition is a remarkable coincidence of dates.

The Second Battle of El-Alamein ended on **4 November 1942**, with Rommel's forces in full retreat. Just **one week later**, on **11 November 1942**, the Greek Orthodox and Roman Catholic Church celebrate the **Feast of Saint Menas**.

**Patriarch Christophoros II** of Alexandria — the Greek Orthodox Patriarch in office throughout the war — chose to mark that feast day in 1942 with a solemn **thanksgiving liturgy** at the Church of Saint Savvas in Alexandria. He publicly gave thanks for the Allied victory, which, in the tradition of his church, he attributed to the intercession of Saint Menas.

Patriarch Christophoros II (1876–1967) was a real historical figure who genuinely served as Patriarch from 1939 to 1966. His celebration of the feast on 11 November 1942 is a historically plausible event — it was his liturgical duty to mark the feast, and the timing of the victory would naturally have given that year's celebration a very particular character. Whether he made a formal declaration about the miracle, or simply offered thanksgiving in the context of the feast, is something the sources do not allow us to determine precisely. But the fact that the Patriarch marked the feast day in the immediate aftermath of the battle is a key part of how the tradition has been framed and remembered.

## 8. How the Story Was Kept Alive

Stories do not simply exist — they are told, retold, written down, and shared. The miracle tradition of Saint Minas at El-Alamein has travelled through history by several routes:

**Oral tradition (1942 onwards):** The Greek and Coptic communities of Egypt — soldiers, clergy, and civilians — carried the story in conversation and sermon, especially on 11 November each year, the feast of Saint Menas.

**Archimandrite Charalambos Vassilopoulos:** A prolific Greek Orthodox clerical writer, he published *The Victorious in El Alamein* in Greek. This book appears to be the earliest written codification of the miracle account, fixing its key details — the midnight apparition, the camels, the Patriarch's thanksgiving — into a transmissible text. It is the source most later accounts draw on.

**Coptic devotion through Pope Kyrillos VI:** On the Coptic side, the story gained a powerful institutional anchor through **Pope Kyrillos VI** (Coptic Pope 1959–1971), whose own monastic name was "Mena." He had a lifelong devotion to Saint Minas and established a **Monastery of Saint Mina** near Lake Mariout in 1959 — in the same desert region as Abu Mena, not far from the El-Alamein battlefield. His personal connection to the saint brought the miracle tradition into the heart of Coptic spiritual life.

**The internet age:** A dedicated English-language website (*stminaalamein.com*) and a widely-read 2010 blog post by Orthodox writer John Sanidopoulos (*johnsanidopoulos.com*) brought the story to global English-speaking Orthodox and Catholic audiences. The Sanidopoulos post has been copied across dozens of religious websites and is now the most widely circulated English-language account.

## 9. History, Legend, and Faith: How to Read This Story

This section is perhaps the most important in the document — because the miracle of Saint Minas sits at the intersection of two different ways of understanding the world, and a thoughtful reader needs tools to navigate that intersection.

### What is historically confirmed

| Claim | Status |
|-------|--------|
| The Second Battle of El-Alamein was fought 23 Oct – 4 Nov 1942 | Confirmed |
| The 1st Greek Brigade participated and suffered casualties | Confirmed |
| Abu Mena (Saint Menas's burial site) is in the same Western Desert region | Confirmed |
| Patriarch Christophoros II was in office in 1942 | Confirmed |
| The feast of Saint Menas falls on 11 November | Confirmed |
| A thanksgiving liturgy on 11 November 1942 is historically plausible | Plausible |
| Archimandrite Vassilopoulos wrote a book recording the tradition | Confirmed |
| Pope Kyrillos VI had a special devotion to Saint Minas | Confirmed |

### What cannot be verified from secular sources

| Claim | Status |
|-------|--------|
| Saint Minas appeared at midnight leading camels toward the German camp | No secular corroboration |
| German soldiers fled in supernatural terror because of the apparition | No secular corroboration |
| Montgomery had a dream in which a figure named "Mena" appeared | Not found in Montgomery's memoirs |
| The Egyptian Gazette reported the miracle on 10 November 1942 | Unverified — claim repeated across religious sites but no one has quoted the actual text; physical microfilm exists at the British Library but has not been examined |
| The Allies gifted land near El-Alamein to the Patriarchate | Not confirmed; monastery built in 1959 through other means |

### Two kinds of truth

When we say something is not historically verifiable, we are not saying it did not happen, nor that it does not matter. We are saying that the methods historians use — documentary evidence, multiple independent sources, physical records — cannot confirm it.

Religious communities have a different relationship to stories like this one. For Greek Orthodox and Coptic Christians, this is not a legend from the distant past. It is a living testimony to the reality of the saints' continuing intercession in human affairs — as real and as relevant as any military history. The story is retold every year on 11 November because it is believed to be true in the fullest sense.

This presents a genuine and important question for anyone studying history: **what is a source?** A military history book and a hagiographic tradition (a tradition about the lives and miracles of saints) are both *sources* — but they work by different rules, carry different kinds of authority, and serve different purposes. Neither is simply "right" or "wrong."

What we can say with confidence is that communities of faith turned to the story of Saint Minas to make meaning out of a moment of terrible danger and unexpected deliverance. The Greek soldiers fighting at El-Alamein were far from home, their country was occupied, and the battle they were fighting that October night would determine the fate of Egypt, the Middle East, and — in some measure — the whole war. In that extremity, the presence of a soldier-saint who had walked the same desert seventeen centuries before was not a comforting fantasy. It was, for many of them, the deepest reality they knew.

## 10. The Legacy: Abu Mena After the War

The battle of El-Alamein gave Abu Mena a renewed significance in both the Greek Orthodox and Coptic traditions.

**Pope Kyrillos VI** (born Azer Youssef Atta, 1902–1971) became one of the most beloved Coptic Popes of the twentieth century. Long before his election as Pope, he had lived as a monk with a special devotion to Saint Minas. After becoming Pope in 1959, he established the **Monastery of Saint Mina** in the Mariout desert — in the same sacred landscape as Abu Mena — partly as an expression of the wartime gratitude and religious renewal that the El-Alamein tradition had energised. The monastery continues to function today as a living Coptic community.

The Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Alexandria has also maintained a connection to the area. The Greek War Cemetery at El-Alamein, where the fallen soldiers of the 1st Greek Brigade are buried, remains a place of annual commemoration for the Greek community of Egypt and the Greek diaspora.

Abu Mena itself was inscribed as a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** in 1979. Despite serious conservation challenges — the ancient site has been damaged by waterlogging from surrounding farmland — it remains a place of pilgrimage and an internationally recognised monument of early Christian civilisation. UNESCO announced in 2025 that it had been removed from the List of World Heritage in Danger following successful restoration work.

Every year on **11 November**, Greek Orthodox churches around the world mark the feast of Saint Minas. In many communities, the El-Alamein miracle is read as part of the liturgical commemoration — a modern chapter in the long story of a saint whose reach has always extended far beyond the desert where he was buried.

## 11. Key Terms Glossary

| Term | Definition |
|------|------------|
| **Ampulla (pl. ampullae)** | Small clay pilgrim flasks, stamped with the image of Saint Menas, that pilgrims filled with holy water or oil and carried home from Abu Mena |
| **Ascetic** | A person who withdraws from ordinary life to live in prayer, fasting, and solitude |
| **Abu Mena** | Arabic: "Father Mena" — the desert pilgrimage site in northwestern Egypt where Saint Menas was buried and venerated |
| **Apparition** | A supernatural appearance of a holy figure, seen by believers |
| **Archimandrite** | A senior title in the Greek Orthodox Church, roughly equivalent to a senior monk or abbot |
| **Coptic Orthodox Church** | The ancient Christian church of Egypt, tracing its origins to the apostle Mark; the Coptic Pope is based in Cairo |
| **Diaspora** | A community of people living outside their ancestral homeland (e.g., the Greek diaspora in Egypt) |
| **Feast day** | In Christian tradition, the annual day on which a saint is commemorated |
| **Hagiography** | Writing about the lives and miracles of saints; a distinct literary and religious genre |
| **Intercession** | In Christian belief, the act of a saint praying to God on behalf of living believers |
| **Martyr** | A person who dies for their religious faith rather than renounce it |
| **Patriarch** | The senior bishop of an Eastern Christian church; the Patriarch of Alexandria leads the Greek Orthodox Church in Egypt and Africa |
| **Patriarchate of Alexandria** | The Greek Orthodox institution based in Alexandria, Egypt, one of the oldest Christian institutions in the world |
| **Phrygia** | An ancient region in west-central Anatolia (modern Turkey), where Saint Menas was stationed and martyred |
| **Primary source** | A first-hand account or document created at the time of the events described |
| **Secondary source** | An account written later, drawing on primary sources and other evidence |
| **UNESCO World Heritage Site** | A place recognised by the United Nations as having outstanding universal cultural or natural value |
| **Wonderworker** | An honorary title given by the Orthodox Church to saints believed to have performed miraculous healings |

## 12. Discussion Questions (for Classroom Use)

1. **History and religious tradition:** The miracle of Saint Minas is recorded in religious texts and oral tradition, not in secular military history. Does that mean it did not happen? What does it mean to say something is "historically unverifiable"?

2. **Why this story?** The Greek and Coptic communities in Egypt experienced the Second Battle of El-Alamein as something that could directly affect their lives — Alexandria was just over 100 km from the front lines. How might the danger of the moment have shaped the way people interpreted what happened?

3. **Symbols and meaning:** The image of Saint Minas — a soldier-saint, leading camels through the desert — is a very specific one. Why do you think that particular image would be meaningful to soldiers and civilians in 1942, in a desert that the saint himself had walked seventeen centuries earlier?

4. **The role of coincidence:** The feast of Saint Menas falls on 11 November — just one week after the battle ended on 4 November. If the battle had ended in August or March, do you think the miracle tradition would have developed in the same way? What does this suggest about how religious meaning is made?

5. **Sources and authority:** Archimandrite Vassilopoulos's book, the Egyptian Gazette citation, and Patriarch Christophoros II's thanksgiving liturgy are different kinds of sources. Research shows that no one has ever actually quoted the supposed Egyptian Gazette article — the claim has been repeated for decades without anyone checking the original newspaper, which sits on microfilm at the British Library in London. What does this tell us about how secondary sources can be copied and treated as facts? How would you go about verifying it?

6. **The Montgomery dream:** The claim that Montgomery had a dream about a figure named "Mena" appears on a devotional website but not in Montgomery's own memoirs. How should we treat a claim that comes from a secondary source but cannot be traced to a primary source?

7. **Parallel traditions:** The research found that a separate Jewish tradition *also* attaches miraculous significance to El-Alamein — describing how the prayers of rabbis in the Holy Land helped stop Rommel from reaching Jewish communities in Palestine. What does the existence of multiple, independent religious miracle narratives about the same battle tell us?

8. **Heritage and memory:** Abu Mena is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site and an active monastery. The Greek War Cemetery at El-Alamein is maintained as a place of national commemoration. In what ways do communities use physical places to keep a story alive? Why does it matter that there are buildings, not just books?
